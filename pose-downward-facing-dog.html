Downward-Facing Dog Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

An inverted V that loads the shoulders and lengthens the back line of the legs. Bent knees are the normal starting version, not a compromise.

Jump to the breakdown

Photograph via Unsplash

The shape, in plain language

Downward-Facing Dog is probably the most recognizable posture in modern yoga and one of the least well explained. It is usually described as a stretch for the back of the legs, which is only half of what is going on. It is at least as much a shoulder posture: the arms are overhead relative to the torso, and they are carrying a meaningful part of your body weight while they are up there.

That combination is why it is hard for beginners in two completely different ways. If your hamstrings are short relative to your leg length, the pelvis cannot tip forward far enough and the lower back rounds. If your shoulders do not yet have the range to bring the arms fully overhead, the upper back rounds instead. Both problems look like the same slumped shape from the outside and they need opposite fixes.

It is described as a leg stretch. It is at least as much a shoulder posture, and that is why it is hard for two entirely different reasons. Downward-Facing Dog

The fix for the first is straightforward and permanent: bend the knees. Bend them a lot. A version with knees bent forty-five degrees and a long spine is a better Downward-Facing Dog than a version with straight legs and a rounded back, and it will always be the better version. Heels do not need to touch the floor. Many people with long femurs or short calves will never put their heels down, and there is nothing to fix there.

The fix for the second is to use a wall or a chair, which reduces the load in the arms and lets the shoulders find the overhead position without carrying weight at the same time. Both of those variations are listed below as full alternatives.

Downward-Facing Dog Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

Level Beginner Hold 30 to 60 seconds Breath Even, five to eight breaths Props None. Wall, chair or blocks help a great deal.

Entry steps

  1. Start on hands and knees with the hands about shoulder-width apart and the knees about hip-width apart, hips stacked over the knees.
  2. Walk the hands forward about six inches and spread the fingers wide, with the middle finger pointing straight ahead and the whole palm in contact with the floor.
  3. Tuck the toes under and press evenly through the hands.
  4. Lift the knees away from the floor and send the hips up and back. Keep the knees generously bent at this stage.
  5. Lengthen the spine first: reach the sitting bones up and back until the back feels long from the tailbone to the base of the neck.
  6. Only once the spine is long, begin to straighten the legs as far as they will go without the low back rounding again. Stop at that point, whatever it is.
  7. Let the head hang between the upper arms so the neck is not holding anything, and press the floor away from you through the base of the index fingers.

Alignment cues

  • Long spine first, straight legs second. If you have to choose, choose the spine every time.
  • Press the floor away. The instruction is to push down through the hands, not to hang from the shoulders.
  • Weight forward into the index finger mounds. Most people dump weight into the outer edge of the palm and the wrist complains.
  • Turn the upper arms outward slightly. A small external rotation gives the shoulder joint more room overhead.
  • Heels do not need to reach the floor. Reaching them down is useful; landing them is optional and often anatomically unavailable.

Breath pattern

Five to eight even breaths through the nose. The shape naturally restricts the belly a little, so most of the movement will be felt in the ribs and the back. If the breath becomes ragged or you start holding it, that is the signal to come down, not to push on. Move into the shape on an exhale and come out of it on an exhale.

Suggested hold

Thirty to sixty seconds to start, which is roughly five to eight breaths. Practitioners who have been at it a while sometimes hold for two or three minutes, but the shoulders tire faster than people expect. Come down before the shape falls apart rather than after.

Common mistakes

  • Straightening the legs before the spine is long. This is the single most common error and it turns the posture into a rounded-back hang.
  • Hands too close to the feet. The shape wants length. If it feels crowded, walk the hands three inches further forward.
  • Gripping with the fingers and floating the palm. The whole hand should be down. Wrist discomfort usually starts here.
  • Holding the head up to look forward. Let the neck go. Looking forward compresses the back of the neck for no benefit.
  • Treating it as a resting posture. It is often used as one in class, but it is genuinely demanding. Rest in Child's Pose instead.

Accessible modifications, each a full alternative

  • At the wall. Stand facing a wall, place both palms flat on it at roughly hip height, and walk your feet back until the arms are straight and the torso is horizontal. You get almost all of the shoulder work with none of the weight in the hands, and you can bend the knees as much as you like. This is the version we recommend for most first practices.
  • Hands on a chair. Set a sturdy chair against a wall so it cannot slide, place both hands on the seat, and walk the feet back until the torso is roughly horizontal. It sits between the wall version and the floor version in difficulty and is a good choice if getting down to the floor is not straightforward.
  • Blocks under the hands. Place a block at its tallest setting under each hand on the floor. Raising the hands four inches gives the pelvis more room to tip forward and often lets the spine lengthen immediately. It also reduces the angle at the wrist, which helps if the wrists are sensitive.
  • Forearms down (Dolphin). Come onto the forearms instead of the hands, elbows shoulder-width, forearms parallel. This removes the wrists from the equation entirely and asks more of the shoulders. Keep the knees bent generously here.
  • Knees bent throughout. A permanently bent-knee version is a complete posture in its own right and is the correct long-term choice for a great many people. Nothing about it is provisional.

Progression

  • Hold for longer before the shape degrades: build from thirty seconds to a minute, then to ninety seconds.
  • Pedal the feet slowly, bending one knee at a time, which moves the calves and ankles through range.
  • Lift one leg a few inches without letting the hips rotate open, then swap. This asks a great deal more of the standing side.
  • Move into Warrior II or a lunge from here, which is how it is used in most flowing sequences.

Approach with care, or skip

  • Approach with care if you have wrist pain or a wrist condition; use the forearm version or the wall version, both of which remove the wrist entirely.
  • Approach with care if you have a shoulder injury or restricted overhead range. The wall version lets you work without load.
  • Head-below-heart positions are not appropriate for everyone. If you have any condition affecting blood pressure, the eyes or the inner ear, or if you experience dizziness, use the wall or chair version and speak with a qualified healthcare professional.
  • In later pregnancy this shape is often uncomfortable; the wall version with a wide stance is usually preferred. Ask your own healthcare professional.
  • Come out immediately if you feel any pain in the wrist, shoulder or low back rather than broad lengthening in the legs.

This is general guidance, not an assessment of you. Work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance, and speak with a qualified healthcare professional before beginning any new physical practice. Read the health and safety disclaimer.

Where this posture shows up

It appears in the 10-Minute Morning as a single thirty-second hold, and three times in the Gentle Full Practice, where the wall version is listed as the beginner path throughout.

In a vinyasa class you will pass through this shape dozens of times, usually briefly. In a hatha or Iyengar-influenced class you are more likely to hold it and be given detailed instructions about the hands and shoulders. See styles explained for what to expect from each.

If you only ever practice the wall version, you are still practicing the posture. The wall is not a waiting room.
