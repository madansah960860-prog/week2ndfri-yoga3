Breathwork basics

Counting the breath, and nothing more than that.

What happens mechanically when you breathe, why classes ask you to count it, three patterns you will meet, and an on-screen pacer to practice with.

Photograph via Unsplash

What is actually happening

Breathing is a mechanical event before it is anything else. The diaphragm, a dome-shaped sheet of muscle sitting under the lungs, contracts and flattens downward. That increases the volume of the chest cavity, pressure drops, and air moves in. The lower ribs swing outward and slightly upward at the same time, and the contents of the abdomen are displaced forward and to the sides, which is why the belly moves. On the exhale the diaphragm relaxes back up into its dome and the ribs return, and air moves out.

That is the whole mechanism. When a yoga teacher asks you to breathe into your back, or into your side ribs, they are asking you to notice a part of that movement you are probably not attending to. Nothing new is happening; the instruction is about attention.

Counted breathing in a class serves two practical purposes. It gives a group of people a shared tempo so that everybody moves at the same speed, and it gives you a task to hold your attention while you are in a position that is otherwise quite boring. Both of those are real and neither of them requires any claim beyond that.

The instruction to breathe into your back is not asking you to do something new. It is asking you to notice something you already do. Breathwork basics

Nose or mouth

Most classes in the United States suggest breathing in and out through the nose. The practical reasons are that nasal breathing naturally slows the breath down, that it makes a faint sound which is easy to keep time with, and that it is harder to breathe raggedly through the nose than through the mouth.

It is a convention, not a requirement. If your nose is blocked, if you have any condition that makes nasal breathing difficult, or if it simply makes you feel short of air, breathe through your mouth. A breath you can sustain comfortably is more useful than one you are fighting.

The one rule

Never force it, and never continue a breathing pattern that makes you feel light-headed, dizzy, breathless or anxious. Stop, breathe normally, and sit down. If any of those things happen repeatedly, stop practicing counted breathing and speak with a qualified healthcare professional.

What this page does not claim

This site makes no medical, therapeutic or health claims about breathing. Counted breathing is described here as a timing tool and an attention task, which is what it is in a yoga class. Nothing on this page treats, prevents or improves any condition.

If you have any respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological or psychiatric condition, or if you are pregnant, speak with a qualified healthcare professional before practicing any counted breathing pattern.

The pacer

A visual count. The bar fills on the inhale and empties on the exhale. Choose a pattern, press start, and follow it for as long as it is comfortable. Nothing is recorded.

Ready

Paused

Start with the even four count. If four is a strain, use the three count. Stop the moment you feel light-headed or short of air, and breathe normally.

Three patterns you will meet in a class

Described mechanically. No claims are made about what any of them do beyond setting a tempo.

Even count

Four in, four out

The default. Inhale for a count of four, exhale for a count of four, with no pause at either end. It is the pattern used to time movement in most classes, because it divides evenly into the up and down of a Cat and Cow or a lift and lower.

Use it for. Moving with the breath, and for any position where you want a steady tempo.

Longer exhale

Four in, six out

Inhale for four, exhale for six. The exhale is the passive half of the cycle in ordinary breathing, so lengthening it usually feels easier than lengthening the inhale. It slows the overall rate without asking for more air.

Use it for. Long held positions, and the supported shapes in the Evening Wind-Down.

Four-part count

Four in, four hold, four out, four hold

An even count with a pause at the top and the bottom. It requires more attention than the other two and is more likely to feel like a strain, so approach it carefully and abandon it the moment it stops being comfortable.

Use it for. Seated practice only. Do not use a holding pattern while moving.

Stop if

Stop any counted breathing pattern immediately and return to normal breathing if you feel light-headed, dizzy, breathless, tingly in the hands or face, or anxious. Sit or lie down until it passes. Do not practice breath holding while driving, in water, or while standing.

Counted breathing is not appropriate for everyone. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before practicing it, particularly if you are pregnant or have any respiratory, cardiovascular, neurological or psychiatric condition. Read the health and safety disclaimer.

Words you will hear

Prāṇāyāma. The general term for breathing practices in yoga. In a US studio it usually refers to a few minutes of counted breathing at the start or end of a class.

Ujjāyī. A way of breathing through the nose with a slight narrowing at the back of the throat, which produces a soft audible sound. It is used in flowing classes to give the room a shared tempo. If it makes your throat sore, stop doing it.

Sama vṛtti. An even count, the same length in and out.

Viṣama vṛtti. An uneven count, most commonly a longer exhale than inhale.

Kumbhaka. A pause between the inhale and the exhale. Approach with care and skip it entirely if you are pregnant or have any relevant condition.

More terms, with pronunciation notes, sit in the glossary.

How to set a seat

Sit on the front edge of a folded blanket or a firm cushion so the hips are higher than the knees, which lets the pelvis tip slightly forward and the spine stay long without effort. An ordinary dining chair with both feet flat on the floor works just as well and is the better choice for most people. Hands anywhere comfortable. Eyes open or closed.
