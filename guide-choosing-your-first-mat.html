A mat is a floor. That is the whole job.

Thickness, grip, length and material, what actually changes between a $25 mat and a $120 one, and when the difference is worth paying for.

Choosing a first mat without overthinking it

Published 09/08/2026  ·  10 minutes read  ·  By the editors of Stillpoint

A yoga mat has exactly two jobs. It stops your hands and feet from sliding, and it puts a small amount of padding between you and a hard floor. Every other property a mat can have is a refinement of one of those two things, and most of the marketing attached to mats is about neither.

This matters because the market has an enormous price range, from about $15 to well over $150, and the difference between the ends of that range is much smaller than the difference in price suggests. Somewhere around $30 a mat becomes entirely adequate. Above about $80 you are buying durability, a specific surface texture, and in some cases a material preference. None of those change what happens in a practice on your first day.

The four things that actually vary

Thickness

Measured in millimeters, and the single most consequential number. Standard is 4 to 5 mm and it is standard because it works for almost everything.

  • 2 to 3 mm. Thin. Excellent stability for standing balance because the floor is almost directly under you. Uncomfortable for kneeling. Travel mats live here.
  • 4 to 5 mm. The default, and the right answer for a first mat. Enough padding for kneeling, firm enough for Tree Pose.
  • 6 mm and above. Comfortable for floor work and noticeably worse for balance, because the surface compresses unevenly under your foot. Worth considering if most of your practice is the Evening Wind-Down and almost none of it is standing.

If your knees are the limiting factor rather than the mat, the cheaper fix is a folded blanket under them rather than a thicker mat under all of you.

Above about eighty dollars you are buying durability and surface texture. Neither changes what happens on your first day. Choosing a first mat

Grip

How much your hands slide when they are damp. This is where cheap mats most obviously differ from expensive ones, and how much you care depends almost entirely on whether your hands sweat and whether you practice anything vigorous.

If your practice is the 10-Minute Morning and the Desk Reset, grip barely matters. If you intend to take warm Vinyasa classes, it matters a great deal, and it is the one property genuinely worth paying for. A cheap towel laid over a cheap mat solves most of the problem for about $15.

Length and width

Standard US mats are 68 inches long and 24 inches wide. If you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches, your heels will hang off the end when you lie down, which is mildly annoying in a final rest and irrelevant the rest of the time. Longer mats at 72 or 84 inches exist and are worth it above roughly 6 feet.

Wider mats at 26 to 30 inches are worth considering if you have broad shoulders or if you find yourself running out of mat sideways in a wide stance.

Material

PVC is the cheap standard: durable, grippy enough, not biodegradable. TPE is lighter and softer, less durable, more recyclable. Natural rubber grips best when damp, weighs the most, is expensive, and has a strong smell for the first few weeks. Cork over rubber grips better as it gets wetter, which is unusual and genuinely useful, and costs the most.

If you have a latex allergy, avoid natural rubber and cork-over-rubber and check the listing carefully.

What we would buy

  • A first mat, $25 to $40. A 4 to 5 mm PVC or TPE mat, 68 inches, from any general sporting goods retailer. It will last two or three years of regular home use. Browse mats at REI or Dick's Sporting Goods.
  • If your hands sweat, add $15. A cotton or microfiber mat towel over the mat. Cheaper and more effective than upgrading the mat itself.
  • If you are over 6 feet, spend a little more. A 72 or 84 inch mat costs roughly $20 more and removes a small permanent irritation.
  • If you already know you will practice for years, $80 to $120. A natural rubber or cork-topped mat lasts five years or more and grips considerably better. This is a reasonable purchase after six months of consistent practice, not before.

What we would not buy, at least not first

  • A starter kit. Usually a thin mat, a foam block and a strap of poor quality, bundled at a price that looks like a discount. Buy the mat separately and see the props guide for the household substitutes that cost nothing.
  • An alignment-printed mat. The printed lines assume a body of a particular proportion and encourage you to place your feet where the mat says rather than where your hips want them. Read how to read an alignment cue instead.
  • A mat bag. Most mats come with a strap or can be carried under one arm. If you are practicing at home, it will live rolled in a corner.
  • A second mat "for travel". Buy one mat. Decide about travel later, if travel ever becomes the reason you are not practicing, which it almost certainly will not.

You may not need one at all

A rug that does not slide on a hard floor works for every sequence on this site. A dense low-pile carpet works for seated and lying practice and is poor for standing balance. If you are not sure whether a home practice is going to stick, practice on what you already own for two weeks first. If it does stick, you will have a much better sense of what you actually need, and the $30 will be a considerably better-informed $30.

The only surface genuinely worth avoiding is a smooth hard floor with nothing on it, particularly in socks. A slip is the most likely way to get hurt in a home practice and it is entirely preventable.

Looking after it

Wipe it down with a damp cloth and a little mild soap when it gets dirty, and let it dry flat or hanging rather than rolled. Do not put it in a washing machine unless the manufacturer says so, and do not leave it in a hot car, which will warp most materials permanently. Store it rolled with the top surface facing outward so the corners do not curl up.

That is the entire maintenance requirement. A mat is a floor. It does not need much.

The one-line answer

A 4 to 5 mm mat, 68 inches long, in the $25 to $50 range. Buy longer if you are over 5 feet 10 inches. Everything else is preference.
