Bridge Pose Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

Lying on your back and lifting the hips with the feet planted. The back of the legs works, the front of the hips lengthens, and there is a supported version you can rest in.

Jump to the breakdown

Photograph via Unsplash

The shape, in plain language

Bridge Pose is unusual in that it has two quite different personalities depending on whether you hold yourself up or let a prop hold you up. The active version is a leg and hip posture: the back of the thighs and the muscles around the hips work to lift and hold the pelvis, and the front of the hips lengthens because the thighs are moving away from the torso. The supported version, with a block under the sacrum, asks nothing of the legs at all and is used at the end of a practice as a resting shape.

Both are worth knowing, and they belong at different points in a sequence. The active version sits in the middle of a practice while there is still energy for it. The supported version belongs near the end, alongside the other low, quiet shapes.

One posture with two personalities: hold yourself up and it is leg work, let a block hold you up and it is a place to rest. Bridge Pose

The single most useful detail concerns the feet. They should be about hip-width apart and close enough to the hips that you could brush your heels with your fingertips. Feet too far away turns the posture into a hamstring cramp; feet too close crowds the knees. Parallel is the goal, and the feet want to stay parallel rather than turning outward as the hips lift.

The second useful detail is that the lift is not a spinal curl. Think of the pelvis being carried upward by the legs rather than the low back being arched. If you feel it as a pinch in the lower back rather than work in the legs, lower a couple of inches and press the feet down harder.

Bridge Pose Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

Level Beginner Hold 30 to 45 seconds, or 3 to 5 minutes supported Breath Inhale to lift, exhale to lower Props None active. Block and blanket for the supported version.

Entry steps

  1. Lie on your back with the knees bent and the feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart and parallel.
  2. Walk the heels close enough that your fingertips can just brush them when the arms are alongside the body.
  3. Let the arms rest alongside the torso, palms down, and lengthen the back of the neck so the chin is not tipped up.
  4. Press evenly through both feet, particularly through the base of the big toe, and let the pelvis begin to lift away from the floor.
  5. Continue lifting until the hips are as high as they will go without the lower back complaining and without the knees splaying outward.
  6. Press the arms and the backs of the shoulders into the floor. Some people can interlace the fingers underneath and roll onto the outer shoulders; if that is not comfortable, leave the arms as they are.
  7. To come down, lower the hips slowly and evenly rather than dropping, and rest for a few breaths before repeating.

Alignment cues

  • Feet parallel and hip-width. If the feet turn out, the knees follow and the posture loses its shape.
  • Press the feet down to lift the hips. The lift is a consequence of pressing, not an effort of the low back.
  • Knees track over the ankles. Watch that they do not drift wider as you lift.
  • Back of the neck long. Do not turn the head while the hips are lifted.
  • Height is not the goal. The best version is the one where the work is in the legs and the low back is quiet.

Breath pattern

Inhale as you lift, exhale as you lower. If you are holding, breathe evenly for five to eight breaths in the lifted position. In the supported version with a block under the sacrum there is no active breath pattern at all: let it slow down on its own and stay for three to five minutes. Moving up and down with the breath, six or eight times, is a useful alternative to holding.

Suggested hold

Thirty to forty-five seconds for the active lifted version, repeated two or three times with a rest between. The supported version, with a block under the sacrum, can be held for three to five minutes because you are not holding yourself up at all.

Common mistakes

  • Feet too far from the hips. Causes hamstring cramping and makes the lift much harder than it needs to be.
  • Knees splaying outward. Usually means the feet have turned out. A block held between the knees fixes it instantly.
  • Pushing for height and pinching the low back. Lower two inches. Nobody is measuring.
  • Turning the head while lifted. The neck is loaded in this position; keep it still and look straight up.
  • Dropping down at the end. Lower with control, one vertebra at a time if you can.

Accessible modifications, each a full alternative

  • Supported on a block. Lift the hips, slide a block under the bony back of the pelvis at its lowest or middle height, and rest your entire weight on it. The legs stop working completely. This is a restorative shape that can be held for three to five minutes and is the version used in the Evening Wind-Down.
  • Blanket under the shoulders. Fold a blanket and place it under the shoulders and upper back with the head off the edge, which reduces the angle at the neck. Useful for anyone with neck sensitivity.
  • Block between the knees. Hold a block between the thighs just above the knees and squeeze it lightly. This stops the knees drifting apart and gives the inner thighs something to do.
  • Feet on a chair seat. Rest the heels on the seat of a sturdy chair with the knees bent at about ninety degrees, then lift the hips a few inches. Much less range is required and the shape is easy to control.
  • Small lift only. Lift the hips only two or three inches and hold. The leg work is identical and there is far less demand on the front of the hips and the low back.

Progression

  • Repeat the lift and lower six to eight times on the breath before holding, which warms the legs first.
  • Hold for a full minute with the arms pressed down and the hips steady.
  • Interlace the hands beneath you and roll slightly onto the outer edges of the shoulders, which asks more of the upper back.
  • Lift one foot a few inches off the floor for two or three breaths at a time, keeping the pelvis completely level.

Approach with care, or skip

  • Approach with care with any neck condition or neck injury. Do not turn the head while the hips are lifted, and consider the blanket-under-the-shoulders version or skip the posture.
  • Approach with care with low back conditions. Lift less, press the feet more, and stop if you feel a pinch rather than work in the legs.
  • Approach with care after any shoulder injury, since the arms and shoulders are pressed into the floor.
  • Lying flat on the back is not comfortable or advisable for everyone during pregnancy, particularly after the first trimester. Speak with your own healthcare professional about what is appropriate for you.
  • If you feel any pain in the low back, the neck or the knees, come down immediately and use the supported block version instead.

Where this posture shows up

The active version appears in the Gentle Full Practice as three repeated lifts followed by a hold. The supported version closes the Evening Wind-Down before the final rest.

In a class you will often meet Bridge near the end of the floor sequence, frequently just before or just after a twist. It is also commonly offered as an alternative for practitioners who are not doing shoulderstand.

If you only take one version from this entry, take the supported one. It costs a single block and it is the shape people are most likely to actually use at home.
