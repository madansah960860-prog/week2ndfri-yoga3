A cue is a direction, not a destination.

What teachers mean by stacking, grounding, lengthening, engaging and softening, and how to tell when a cue does not apply to your proportions.

Photograph via Unsplash

How to read an alignment cue without obeying it blindly

Published 09/08/2026  ·  12 minutes read  ·  By the editors of Stillpoint

A yoga class is an unusual environment in one specific respect: a person at the front of the room gives an instruction to twenty people with twenty different skeletons, and every one of them is expected to apply it. Most of the time this works, because most cues are directions rather than destinations. Trouble starts when a cue is heard as a destination, which is when people begin forcing a shape their proportions do not offer.

Learning to read a cue is therefore a real skill, and it is one that home practitioners need more than studio practitioners do, because there is nobody in the room to say "not you, you are already there".

Cues are almost always relative

When a teacher says "lengthen your spine", they do not mean reach an absolute length. They mean: from wherever you are now, go a little in that direction. Almost every cue in a yoga class works this way. "Draw the front knee toward the little toe" means more toward the little toe than it currently is. "Soften the shoulders" means less than you are currently holding.

This matters because a cue given to a room is calibrated for the average of that room. If you are already at the far end of the range the cue is pointing toward, applying it takes you past the point that was intended. The person who is already very mobile in the shoulders and hears "open your chest" is the person most likely to end up compressing their lower back.

The five cues you will hear most

Ground, root, press down

All three mean the same thing: push into the floor with a specific part of you. In a standing posture it usually means press through the whole foot rather than just the heel or just the ball. In Downward-Facing Dog it means push the floor away with the hands rather than hang from the shoulders.

It is one of the most reliably useful cues because it is a genuine mechanical instruction with an obvious test: are you pressing, or are you resting? In Warrior II, pressing the outer edge of the back foot down is what stops the whole posture drifting forward, and it is the difference between a stance that holds and one that slides.

Lengthen, extend, reach

This means create distance between two points, usually the pelvis and the head, or the pelvis and the far end of a limb. It is a cue about direction rather than about achieving a straight line.

The trap is that many people execute "lengthen your spine" by lifting the ribcage away from the pelvis, which arches the lower back rather than lengthening the whole spine. A better internal test is whether the distance between the bottom of your ribs and the top of your pelvis has increased at the front and the back equally, or only at the front.

Stack, align, square

Stacking means putting one part directly above another: knee over ankle, shoulder over wrist, ribs over pelvis. It is the most literal family of cues and the easiest to check, which is why teachers use it so often.

It is also the family most likely to be anatomically unavailable. "Square your hips" in a lunge asks the back hip to rotate forward, and the amount of rotation available at the hip joint varies substantially between individuals because of differences in the shape and orientation of the hip socket. Some people will square fully with no effort. Some will never square, at any point, however long they practice. Both are normal. If squaring requires you to strain, the correct response is to square as far as it goes and then stop.

Engage, activate, firm

This means contract a muscle deliberately. "Firm the thighs" in Mountain Pose means contract the quadriceps so the leg is held straight by muscle rather than by the knee joint reaching its end range. "Engage the core" is a vaguer version of the same instruction and usually means firm the abdominal wall slightly rather than brace it hard.

The useful test is whether you can still breathe normally. A muscular engagement that stops the breath is too much for almost any purpose in a general practice.

Soften, release, let go

The counterpart to engage, and much harder to act on because it asks you to notice something you are doing unconsciously. "Soften the jaw" and "release the shoulders" are worth taking seriously because holding in those places is common, costs energy and does nothing useful.

Where it becomes unhelpful is when applied to a place that genuinely needs to be working. Softening in a posture that requires load is how people end up hanging on their joints. If a cue to soften makes a shape feel less stable, it was not meant for that part of you.

Warrior II, cue by cue

Take a typical minute of instruction in Warrior II: "Front knee over the ankle. Track it toward the little toe. Press the outer back foot down. Square the shoulders over the hips. Soften the shoulders down the back. Reach out through both hands. Turn the head and look past the front fingers."

That is seven instructions. Three of them are structural and worth prioritizing: knee over ankle, knee toward the little toe, and outer back foot pressing down. Those three determine whether the posture is stable and whether the knee is loaded sensibly. Two are postural and useful: shoulders over hips, shoulders soft. Two are cosmetic and can be dropped entirely without harming the posture: reaching through the hands and turning the head.

Knowing which are which means that when the room moves faster than you can follow, you know what to keep. If your neck does not enjoy turning, do not turn it. If holding the arms out at shoulder height for a minute is the reason you come out of the shape early, put your hands on your hips and keep the legs. Nobody is grading this.

When a cue does not apply to you

There are three reliable signals that a cue is not for your body on this day.

  • Applying it produces sharp or pinching sensation. Not the broad, spread-out feeling of a muscle lengthening, but something specific and located, especially in a joint. Stop applying the cue.
  • Applying it makes the shape less stable. If following an instruction makes you wobble more or feel like you are about to be tipped over, it was calibrated for someone in a different position than you.
  • Applying it stops the breath. An instruction that requires you to hold your breath to execute is asking for more than the posture needs.

None of those are reasons to distrust the teacher. They are reasons to recognize that a cue given to a room cannot be right for every body in it, and that noticing the mismatch is your job rather than theirs.

The limits of reading this from a page

We should be honest about what a written pose entry can and cannot do. It can tell you what a shape is, what it asks of you, what usually goes wrong and what to do about it. It cannot see your front knee. It cannot notice that your left shoulder rides higher than your right, or that you are holding your breath every time you enter a shape, or that what you are calling a hamstring stretch is happening in your lower back.

That is the specific thing a qualified teacher in a room provides, and it is not replaceable by more detailed writing. If you practice at home, taking an occasional class is the single most useful thing you can add, and it is worth doing even if you never intend to become a regular studio attender. The teacher directory is a place to start, and the styles guide explains which kind of class gives the most instruction per posture.

In the meantime, the three-question check is a reasonable substitute: can you breathe evenly, is the sensation broad rather than sharp, and can you come out of the shape under control. If all three are true, carry on. If any one of them is false, change something.

The worked example

This guide uses Warrior II throughout, because it is the posture that attracts the largest number of simultaneous cues in a typical class.

The three-question check

Can you breathe evenly in the shape? Is the sensation broad rather than sharp? Can you come out of it under control?

If all three are true, you are close enough for a home practice. For anything more precise you need a qualified teacher in the room. See the directory.
