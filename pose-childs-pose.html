Child's Pose Bālāsana

Kneeling with the hips settling back and the torso resting forward. The resting position of the practice, and the one worth learning to set up properly.

Jump to the breakdown

Photograph via Unsplash

The shape, in plain language

Child's Pose is where you go when a practice asks for more than you have that day, and it is the shape most likely to be set up badly precisely because it is treated as a break rather than as a posture. Done well it is a genuinely restful position that can be held for several minutes. Done badly it is a kneeling position that puts pressure on the knees and the ankles while the head hangs somewhere it cannot rest.

The two variables are the width of the knees and the support under the torso. Knees together makes it a compact forward fold with the belly compressed against the thighs. Knees wide, roughly mat-width apart, gives the torso somewhere to go and is more comfortable for most people, including anyone with a larger body and anyone who is pregnant.

It is treated as a break, which is exactly why it is so often set up badly. It is a posture, and it repays being built properly. Child's Pose

The support question is simpler: the head should be resting on something. If the forehead reaches the floor comfortably, the floor is the something. If it does not, stack a block, a folded blanket or a bolster under the forehead until it does. A head that is hovering an inch above the mat is being held up by the neck, and a neck that is working is not resting.

The same logic applies underneath. If the hips do not settle onto the heels, fill the gap with a folded blanket between the calves and the backs of the thighs. If the knees or the tops of the feet are uncomfortable, put a folded blanket under them. Nothing about this is cheating. The posture is the resting, not the reaching.

At a glance

English name. Child's Pose

Sanskrit. Bālāsana

Difficulty. Beginner

Body areas. Hips, spine, shoulders

Suggested hold. 1 to 5 minutes

Props that help. Blanket, bolster and block all help.

Appears in. Morning, Evening Wind-Down, Gentle Full Practice.

Child's Pose Bālāsana

Level Beginner Hold 1 to 5 minutes Breath Slow. Feel it in the back ribs. Props Blanket, bolster and block all help.

Entry steps

  1. Begin on hands and knees with the knees under the hips and the hands under the shoulders.
  2. Bring the big toes to touch behind you and take the knees as wide as is comfortable, up to about the width of your mat.
  3. Sit the hips back toward the heels. If they do not reach, place a folded blanket between the backs of the thighs and the calves and sit on that.
  4. Walk the hands forward and let the torso settle down between or over the thighs.
  5. Rest the forehead on the floor, or on a block, a folded blanket or a bolster, whichever brings the support up to meet your head.
  6. Leave the arms extended forward with the elbows soft, or bring them back alongside the body with the palms facing up, whichever the shoulders prefer.
  7. Stay for one to five minutes and let the breath move into the back of the ribs, which is the only place there is room for it in this shape.

Alignment cues

  • The head rests on something. Floor, block, blanket or bolster. Not on the neck.
  • Knees as wide as is comfortable. Wider gives the torso room; narrower is more compact. Neither is more correct.
  • Fill the gap at the hips. If the hips hover above the heels, put a blanket in the gap.
  • Breathe into the back ribs. The front of the body is compressed here; the back is where the movement is.
  • Shoulders soft. Arms forward or back, but not straining in either direction.

Breath pattern

Slow and unhurried, with no counted pattern. Because the front of the body is folded, the belly cannot expand much, so the breath tends to move into the back and sides of the ribcage. Placing your attention there gives you something specific to notice and is one of the reasons this shape is worth holding for several minutes rather than a few breaths.

Suggested hold

One to five minutes. This is one of the few postures where the long hold is the point rather than an ambition. Supported with a bolster, ten minutes is entirely reasonable. Set a timer so you are not deciding when to stop.

Common mistakes

  • Head hovering above the floor. The single most common error. Build the floor up to meet the head.
  • Ignoring pressure at the knees or ankles. A folded blanket under either one solves it in seconds.
  • Knees together when the belly needs room. Take the knees wide. There is no reason not to.
  • Treating it as a five-second pause. Its value is in the length of the hold. Set a timer.
  • Staying in it when the knees hurt. Knee pain is a reason to use the seated or chair version, not to persist.

Accessible modifications, each a full alternative

  • Bolster under the chest. Place a bolster lengthwise between the thighs so it runs from the pelvis toward the head, and rest the whole torso on it with the head turned to one side. Turn the head to the other side halfway through. This is a fully supported version that can be held for ten minutes and is used in the Evening Wind-Down.
  • Blanket behind the knees. Roll a blanket and place it in the crease behind the knees before sitting back. This reduces how far the knee has to bend and is the first thing to try if kneeling is uncomfortable.
  • Blanket under the ankles. A folded blanket under the fronts of the ankles takes the pressure off the tops of the feet, which is a common complaint and an easy fix.
  • Block under the forehead. A block at any of its three heights under the forehead lets the head rest without the neck working. Turn the block on its side or its end depending on how much height you need.
  • Seated in a chair. Sit toward the front of a sturdy chair with the feet flat and the knees apart, then fold forward and rest the forearms on the thighs or the head on a stack of cushions placed on a table in front of you. The same forward fold with none of the kneeling.

Progression

  • Extend the hold from one minute to three, then to five, using a timer rather than judgment.
  • Walk the hands over to one side to lengthen along the opposite side of the torso, then swap.
  • Move slowly between this and hands and knees on the breath, eight or ten times.
  • Use it as the resting position between the more demanding shapes in the Gentle Full Practice rather than stopping the sequence.

Approach with care, or skip

  • Approach with care with any knee condition or after knee surgery. Use the rolled blanket behind the knees, or use the chair version instead.
  • Approach with care with ankle conditions; a folded blanket under the fronts of the ankles usually resolves the discomfort.
  • Take the knees wide rather than together during pregnancy so there is no compression across the abdomen, and speak with your own healthcare professional about what is appropriate.
  • Head-below-heart positions are not appropriate for everyone. If you have any condition affecting blood pressure, the eyes or the inner ear, use the seated chair version.
  • If you feel pins and needles, numbness or pain in the legs or feet, come out and change the setup rather than waiting for it to pass.

Where this posture shows up

Child's Pose appears in the 10-Minute Morning as the opening shape, in the Evening Wind-Down in its bolster-supported form, and repeatedly through the Gentle Full Practice as the resting position between harder shapes.

In a studio class this is the standing offer whenever you need to stop. A teacher who says "take a child's pose whenever you like" means it literally, and taking it is not a statement about your ability.

It is also the most useful shape on Stillpoint to set up properly once, because you will use it more than any other.
