Practice at home

Ninety square feet is plenty.

What a home practice actually requires: a rectangle of floor, a clear wall, a surface that does not slide, and a time that survives a bad week.

Photograph via Unsplash

How much room you actually need

Lie down on your back and stretch your arms overhead. If nothing hits a wall, a coffee table or a radiator, you have enough space for every sequence on this site. For most adults that is a rectangle roughly 7 feet long and 3 feet wide, which is about 21 square feet. Standing positions want another foot of clearance on each side, so call it 7 feet by 5 feet if you want to be comfortable.

That is smaller than most people assume, and it is smaller than the space a studio gives you. The reason to know the number is that it changes the question from "where can I set up a yoga room" to "where can I move a coffee table for ten minutes", which is a much easier question to answer.

Alongside the rectangle, you want about 4 feet of clear wall. A wall without a radiator, a light switch at shoulder height, a picture rail or a baseboard heater. The wall is used in almost every beginner modification on this site, and having one available changes what is possible more than any prop you could buy.

The question is not where to put a yoga room. It is where to move a coffee table for ten minutes. Practice at home

The surface

In order of preference: a mat on a hard floor, a mat on a low-pile carpet, a rug that does not slide on a hard floor, a dense carpet on its own. What does not work is a smooth hard floor with nothing on it, and what is genuinely unsafe is a smooth hard floor in socks.

Thick carpet is fine for seated and lying work and poor for standing balance, because the surface moves under your foot and the corrections you are trying to feel get absorbed. If your only option is deep carpet, use the wall or a chair for balance positions.

Light and noise

Neither matters as much as the internet suggests. Practice in whatever light is available. A dim room suits the Evening Wind-Down and a bright one suits the 10-Minute Morning, and that is about as far as it goes.

Noise is worth a small amount of management, mostly because a phone notification arriving in the middle of a ninety-second hold is genuinely disruptive. Put the phone on Do Not Disturb and face down. If you are using the timer on this site, the page will keep counting; it pauses only when the tab is hidden, which is deliberate so the clock never lies to you.

The measurements

Minimum floor. 7 ft by 3 ft

Comfortable floor. 7 ft by 5 ft

Clear wall. 4 ft wide, floor to about 5 ft high

Ceiling. Standard 8 ft is enough for everything here. Arms overhead in Mountain Pose is the tallest anything gets.

Standard US mat. 68 in by 24 in

Longer mat. 72 or 84 in, worth it above 5 ft 10 in

The chair option

If none of the above is available, the entire Desk Reset needs one chair and one wall, and every other sequence on this site has a chair path.

Small apartments, shared rooms, and everything else

Practical answers to the situations that actually stop people practicing.

Storing props

A rolled mat stands upright in the corner of a closet or behind a door and takes about 6 inches of floor. Two blocks, a strap and a folded blanket fit in a single canvas bin under a bed or on the bottom shelf of a bookcase.

If storage is the obstacle, use the household substitutes in the props guide instead. Books, a bathrobe belt and a blanket you already own need no storage at all because they already live somewhere.

Sharing the room

A practice does not require privacy, it requires floor. If someone else is in the room, tell them how long you will be and then get on with it. Ten minutes is short enough that this is rarely a negotiation.

If you would rather not be watched, the Evening Wind-Down is entirely floor-based and works in a bedroom with the door closed.

Children and animals

Both will join in. This is not a problem to be solved so much as a fact to be planned around. Practicing at a time when a small child is asleep is the usual answer. Practicing with a cat on the mat is the other one.

The practical concern is balance positions, where something arriving unexpectedly against your standing leg is genuinely destabilizing. Keep a wall or a chair within reach.

Choosing a time that holds

Attach the practice to something that already happens every single day, rather than to a time on a clock. After the coffee is poured. Before the shower. When the laptop closes. A trigger that already exists is far more reliable than an intention.

Morning practice has one structural advantage: fewer things have had the chance to go wrong yet. Evening practice has a different one: you are already home. Neither is better and the argument about which is a waste of energy. Pick the one where the trigger is more reliable.

The longer version of this argument is in building a home practice that survives a busy month.

Following along from a screen

Set the screen at the short edge of your mat, at the end where your head goes in a reclined position, and tilt it up. That way it is visible from standing, from hands and knees, and from lying down without moving it.

Better still, use the print layout. Every sequence page on this site has a print stylesheet that strips out the navigation and keeps the position order, hold times and beginner path. A printed page on the floor beside you does not go to sleep, does not need to be tapped with a sweaty hand, and does not show notifications.

Practicing without a teacher in the room

The one genuine limitation of a home practice is that nobody is watching you. A website cannot see whether your front knee is drifting inward or whether your shoulders are creeping toward your ears, and no amount of written detail fully substitutes for someone in the room saying "not like that, like this".

Three things make that gap smaller. First, read the entry before you practice the shape, rather than trying to read and move at the same time. Second, use the three-question check on every position: can you breathe evenly, is the sensation broad rather than sharp, and can you come out of it under control. Third, take a class with a qualified teacher when you can, even occasionally, and use what you learn there at home. The teacher directory is a starting point.

Practice safely
