Mountain Pose Tāḍāsana

Standing still, weight even across both feet. It looks like doing nothing, and it is the reference position every other standing posture is measured against.

Jump to the breakdown

Photograph via Unsplash

The shape, in plain language

Mountain Pose is the position you are already in when you stand at the sink. What makes it a posture rather than an accident is that you decide where the weight goes, instead of letting a lifetime of habit decide for you. Almost everyone stands with more weight on one foot, more weight toward the heels or the toes, one hip carried slightly higher, and the ribs drifting forward of the pelvis. None of that is a defect. It is simply what happens when nobody is paying attention.

The posture asks you to spread your weight evenly across four points on each foot: the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe, the inner heel and the outer heel. From there the legs are straight but not locked, the pelvis is level, the ribcage sits over the pelvis rather than in front of it, the shoulders release down away from the ears, and the head balances on top without jutting forward.

Standing still is the only posture with enough spare attention left over to notice what you are actually doing. Mountain Pose

It is worth learning first because every standing shape on Stillpoint borrows from it. Warrior II is Mountain Pose with the legs apart and one knee bent. Triangle is Mountain Pose tipped sideways. Tree is Mountain Pose on one leg. If the reference position is vague, everything built on it is vague too.

It is also the posture in which you can most easily notice asymmetry. Stand for a full minute and you will feel one foot gripping more than the other, or one hip sitting further back. That noticing is the whole exercise. You are not required to correct it, and in most cases you should not try to force it.

At a glance

English name. Mountain Pose

Sanskrit. Tāḍāsana

Difficulty. Beginner

Body areas. Legs, feet, spine, balance

Suggested hold. 30 to 60 seconds

Props that help. None. A wall or chair helps.

Appears in. Every standing sequence on this site.

Mountain Pose Tāḍāsana

Level Beginner Hold 30 to 60 seconds Breath Even, unforced Props None. A wall or chair helps.

Entry steps

  1. Stand near the front of your mat with the feet either together or about hip-width apart. Hip-width is a better starting point for most people; feet together is a balance challenge, not a requirement.
  2. Lift and spread your toes, then set them down without gripping. Feel for the four points on each foot: base of the big toe, base of the little toe, inner heel, outer heel.
  3. Rock gently forward and back a quarter of an inch, then side to side, and stop in the middle where the weight feels evenly distributed across both feet.
  4. Straighten the legs without locking the knees back. Leave the smallest possible bend so the knee joint is not carrying the load at end range.
  5. Level the pelvis so it is neither tipped strongly forward nor tucked strongly under, and let the ribcage settle back over it rather than lifting in front of it.
  6. Roll the shoulders up, back and down once, then let the arms hang alongside the body with the palms facing in or slightly forward.
  7. Lengthen the back of the neck by drawing the chin very slightly toward the throat, so the ears sit over the shoulders rather than in front of them.

Alignment cues

  • Weight even, front to back. Most people stand with more weight in the heels. Shifting a fraction forward often makes the whole posture feel more alive.
  • Legs straight, knees unlocked. Locking the knees back is not the same as straightening the legs. Engage the thigh muscles instead.
  • Ribs over pelvis. If you can see your lower front ribs jutting forward when you look down, soften them back a little.
  • Shoulders down, not pinned. The instruction is to release, not to squeeze the shoulder blades together.
  • Crown of the head tall. Think of the top of the skull, not the chin, as the highest point.

Breath pattern

Even and unforced through the nose, if the nose is available. There is no counted pattern here. Let the breath settle into whatever rhythm it wants and simply notice whether the ribs and the belly are both moving. If you want a counted breath to work with, the four-count pattern on the breathwork basics page suits this posture well.

Suggested hold

Thirty to sixty seconds, or five to eight full breaths. Longer holds are useful precisely because nothing is happening: it is the only posture where you have enough spare attention to notice where your weight actually sits.

Common mistakes

  • Locking the knees. The most common one by a wide margin. It looks like a straight leg but loads the joint rather than the muscle. Micro-bend and firm the thighs instead.
  • Standing in a permanent slight lean. Almost everyone favors one side. Rocking side to side before you settle catches it.
  • Gripping the toes. Toes should be spread and quiet, not clawing at the mat. Gripping usually means the weight is too far forward.
  • Lifting the chest by arching the low back. A tall chest built from a compressed low back is not a tall chest. Lengthen upward from the sides of the ribs instead.
  • Treating it as a rest between real poses. It is not a pause. It is where the information is.

Accessible modifications, each a full alternative

  • Against a wall. Stand with the heels about two inches from a wall and the back of the pelvis, the upper back and the back of the head lightly touching it. The wall gives you feedback that no verbal cue can. If the head does not reach the wall without straining, leave it forward. That is a proportion, not a fault.
  • In a chair. Sit toward the front of a sturdy chair with both feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart, and build the posture upward from the sitting bones instead of the feet. Everything above the pelvis is identical. This is the version used throughout the Desk Reset sequence.
  • Feet wider. A stance of eight to twelve inches between the feet is more stable than feet together and is the correct choice if balance is a concern, if you are pregnant, or if you simply find the narrower stance distracting.
  • With a hand on a support. Rest one hand lightly on the back of a chair or a countertop. Keeping a hand available removes the low-level vigilance that makes it hard to notice anything else.

Progression

  • Close your eyes for the last two breaths, which removes visual balance information and makes the feet work harder.
  • Bring the feet fully together, big toes touching, heels a half inch apart, which narrows the base considerably.
  • Rise onto the balls of the feet for three to five seconds at a time, then lower with control. Keep a wall within reach.
  • Move on to Tree Pose, which is this posture on one leg.

Approach with care, or skip

  • If you have any condition affecting balance, dizziness on standing, or vertigo, practice within arm's reach of a wall or a chair, or use the seated version.
  • If standing for a minute at a time is uncomfortable for any reason, use the chair version. It is not a lesser posture.
  • During pregnancy, a wider stance is generally more comfortable and more stable. Speak with your own healthcare professional about what is appropriate for you.
  • If you feel light-headed at any point, come out, sit down, and do not continue.

Where this posture shows up

Mountain Pose opens both the 10-Minute Morning and the Gentle Full Practice, and it appears as the seated reference position throughout the Desk Reset.

In a studio class in the United States you will usually hear it called by both names, and it is often used as the pause point between standing shapes. If a teacher says "come back to the top of your mat", this is the posture they mean.

It is also the posture worth returning to whenever a standing shape feels confusing. Reset here, decide where your weight is, and rebuild from that.
