Warrior II Vīrabhadrāsana II

A wide stance with the front knee bent and the arms level. The interesting question is not the arms. It is where the front knee is pointing.

Jump to the breakdown

Photograph via Unsplash

The shape, in plain language

Warrior II is the posture most people picture when they picture yoga, and it is genuinely useful: it asks the front leg to carry load in a bent position, the back leg to stay long and active, the pelvis to open sideways, and the shoulders to hold the arms out at height while none of the above collapses. That is a lot of instructions at once, which is why it is worth breaking into two decisions.

The first decision is the length of the stance. Too short and the front thigh has nothing to do. Too long and the back foot lifts or the pelvis tips. A workable starting point is a stance where, with the front knee bent, the front shin is roughly vertical and the front knee sits over the ankle rather than in front of it. For most adults that is somewhere between three and four feet between the feet.

Everyone watches the arms. The posture is actually decided at the front knee and the back foot. Warrior II

The second decision is what the front knee does. It should track in roughly the same direction as the middle of the front foot. Left to itself, the knee usually drifts inward, which is both less stable and less comfortable. The cue that fixes it is to press the outer edge of the front foot into the floor and think about drawing the front knee gently toward the little-toe side.

The arms are the least important part. If holding them out at shoulder height for a minute is uncomfortable, put your hands on your hips. The posture does not stop being Warrior II.

At a glance

English name. Warrior II

Sanskrit. Vīrabhadrāsana II

Difficulty. Beginner

Body areas. Legs, hips, shoulders, balance

Suggested hold. 30 to 60 seconds each side

Props that help. None. A chair or a wall helps.

Appears in. Gentle Full Practice, Desk Reset (standing).

Warrior II Vīrabhadrāsana II

Level Beginner Hold 30 to 60 seconds each side Breath Even, five to eight breaths Props None. A chair or a wall helps.

Entry steps

  1. Stand sideways on your mat with the feet three to four feet apart, both feet parallel to the short edge of the mat.
  2. Turn the right foot ninety degrees so the toes point toward the short edge of the mat in front of you. Turn the left foot in slightly, perhaps fifteen degrees, so the left toes angle forward a little.
  3. Line the right heel up with the arch of the left foot. If that feels narrow or unsteady, widen the feet sideways so each foot sits on its own rail.
  4. Press the outer edge of the back foot firmly down and straighten the back leg.
  5. Bend the right knee, sending it toward the little-toe side of the right foot, until the right shin is roughly vertical. Do not let the knee travel past the ankle.
  6. Level the pelvis so both hip points face the long edge of the mat as much as your hips allow, then let the torso stay upright over the middle of the stance rather than leaning toward the front leg.
  7. Extend the arms out to the sides at shoulder height, palms down, and turn the head to look past the front hand.

Alignment cues

  • Front knee toward the little toe. If you look down and the knee hides your big toe, widen it.
  • Weight in the outer edge of the back foot. The back leg is not passive; it is what stops you tipping forward.
  • Torso upright in the middle. The shoulders stack over the pelvis, not over the front thigh.
  • Shoulders down, arms long. Reach out through both hands rather than lifting the arms up.
  • Front shin vertical. If the knee is past the ankle, lengthen the stance instead of bending less.

Breath pattern

Five to eight even breaths on each side. Enter the shape on an exhale. The front thigh will start to burn somewhere around the fourth breath; keep the breath at the same pace anyway, because the moment the breath goes short is usually the moment the alignment goes with it. Come out on an exhale, straighten the front leg, and switch sides.

Suggested hold

Thirty to sixty seconds on each side, and it will feel considerably longer than that in the front thigh. Do both sides for the same length of time even though the second side will feel worse; that is normal and it is not a reason to shorten it.

Common mistakes

  • Front knee collapsing inward. The most common and the most worth fixing. Press the outer front foot down.
  • Stance too short. Produces a knee that travels past the ankle and a thigh that does very little.
  • Leaning the torso over the front leg. The chest drifts forward and the back leg loses its job. Stack the shoulders over the middle of the stance.
  • Back heel lifting. Usually means the stance is too long or the back foot needs to turn in a little more.
  • Shoulders creeping up toward the ears. Widen rather than lift; if the arms tire, lower them to the hips.

Accessible modifications, each a full alternative

  • Seated in a chair. Sit sideways on a sturdy chair with the right thigh along the seat and the right foot flat on the floor with the knee bent. Extend the left leg out to the side with the foot turned in and the sole on the floor. Take the arms out at shoulder height. You get the pelvis, torso and shoulder work without any standing load. This version is used in the Desk Reset.
  • Back foot against a wall. Set the outer edge of the back foot against a wall. The wall gives the back leg something definite to press into, which is exactly what makes the posture stable, and removes any worry about the back foot sliding.
  • Hands on hips. Keep the legs exactly as described and rest the hands on the hips. If shoulder range or endurance is the limiting factor, this lets you work the legs for the full hold without the arms deciding when you stop.
  • Shorter stance, smaller bend. Bring the feet two and a half feet apart and bend the front knee only twenty or thirty degrees. Everything else stays the same. This is the right choice on a first practice and on any day the legs are tired.
  • Block under the back hand. If you are moving from Warrior II toward Extended Side Angle, a block under the lower hand at its tallest setting lets the torso stay long instead of collapsing over the thigh.

Progression

  • Extend the hold to ninety seconds a side without letting the front knee drift inward.
  • Move slowly between Warrior II and Extended Side Angle on the breath, four or five times a side.
  • Add Reverse Warrior: keep the legs and tip the torso back, sliding the back hand down the back leg.
  • Link it into a standing sequence so you enter it from Downward-Facing Dog rather than from standing.

Approach with care, or skip

  • Approach with care with any knee condition or previous knee injury. Bend the front knee less, shorten the stance, and stop if you feel anything sharp in or around the joint.
  • Approach with care with hip conditions or after hip surgery; a narrower stance and a smaller bend are usually more comfortable.
  • If holding the arms out is uncomfortable in the shoulder or the neck, use the hands-on-hips version rather than pushing through it.
  • During pregnancy, a wider sideways stance is generally more comfortable. Speak with your own healthcare professional about what is appropriate.
  • Do not practice this on a slippery floor in socks. The back foot needs grip.

Where this posture shows up

Warrior II appears twice in the Gentle Full Practice, once on each side, held for forty-five seconds, and in its seated chair form in the Desk Reset.

In a US studio class it is nearly always part of a standing series, often linked with Extended Side Angle and Reverse Warrior. Teachers frequently walk the room during this shape because the front knee is easy to correct from the outside.

If the front thigh is burning and the alignment is still good, that is the posture working. If the alignment has gone and you are only enduring, come out and take a shorter stance next time.
