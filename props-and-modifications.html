Props and modifications

A prop is not a downgrade.

What each prop actually does, how it is used in every pose on this site, and what you already own that will do the same job.

Photograph via Unsplash

The principle underneath all of it

A prop is a way of putting the floor where your body needs the floor to be. That is the entire idea, and once you have it, choosing a modification stops being guesswork. If a shape asks your hand to reach something it cannot reach without your spine rounding, raise the thing. If a shape asks a joint to bend further than it comfortably bends, fill the gap. If a shape requires you to hold yourself up for three minutes, give the weight to something else.

Arm length, leg length, torso length, hip socket depth and shoulder range vary enormously between people. Two practitioners doing what looks like the same posture may be at completely different points in their available range, and one of them may need a block where the other needs nothing. Neither of them is doing a lesser version.

That is why every pose entry on this site shows its modifications with the same weight as the standard shape, and why the sequence pages carry a full beginner path rather than a footnote. The prop-supported version is not a step toward the real posture. In many cases it is the version you should keep.

If your hand cannot reach the floor without your spine rounding, the problem is the height of the floor, not the length of your arms. Props and modifications

How to decide which prop to reach for

  • Something is too far away. Raise it. Blocks under the hands, a chair seat under the hands, a wall at hip height.
  • A joint is bending further than it likes. Fill the gap. A rolled blanket behind the knee, a folded blanket under the hips.
  • Something is unsupported and hanging. Put something under it. A block under a knee, a bolster under the thighs.
  • You cannot stay without working. Transfer the weight. A block under the pelvis, a bolster under the torso.
  • Balance is the limiting factor. Add a hand. A wall, a chair back, a countertop.
  • Getting to the floor is the limiting factor. Use the chair path. It is included on every sequence on this site.

The minimum kit

Two blocks, one strap, one blanket. Under $60 in total at US prices, and it covers every modification shown anywhere on this site. Add a bolster only if you intend to do supported work regularly.

Or spend nothing: two hardback books, a bathrobe belt and a blanket you already own do the same jobs.

Six props, one at a time

Listed from the most useful to the most specialized. The last two cost nothing.

The block The single most useful prop there is.

What it actually does

A block raises the floor. That is the whole idea and it is worth stating plainly, because the usual framing, that a block helps you reach the floor, gets it backwards. In a standing forward fold or in Triangle, the problem is not that your arms are too short. It is that the floor is too far away for your spine to stay long while you get there. A block at its tallest height brings the floor up by about nine inches, which is usually the entire difference between a rounded back and a long one.

How it is used on this site

  • Under each hand in a standing forward fold, in Triangle, in Pyramid and in a low lunge.
  • Under the sacrum in a supported Bridge, at its lowest height, so the legs stop working entirely.
  • Under the forehead in Child's Pose when the head does not reach the floor.
  • Under each knee in Bound Angle so the legs are supported rather than hanging.
  • Held between the thighs in Bridge to stop the knees drifting apart.

Household substitute

Two hardback books of roughly equal thickness, or a sturdy shoebox packed tight so it does not crush.

If you are buying one

Two blocks, because almost every use above wants a matching pair. Cork is heavier and more stable; foam is lighter and kinder if you land on it. Standard US size is 9 by 6 by 4 inches, giving you three heights depending on which face you stand it on.

The strap Extends your reach without shortening your spine.

What it actually does

A strap makes your arms longer. In a seated forward fold, looping a strap around the feet and holding the strap lets you keep the spine long and the chest open while still working the back of the legs. Without it, most people round the back to reach the feet, which changes the posture into something else entirely.

How it is used on this site

  • Around the feet in a seated forward fold, held with both hands.
  • Around the arch of one foot in a reclined leg stretch, so the leg can lift without the shoulders leaving the floor.
  • Around both feet in Happy Baby, held instead of reaching for the toes.
  • Around the upper arms, just above the elbows, to stop them splaying in forearm work.
  • Looped and held behind the back in a shoulder stretch when the hands do not meet.

Household substitute

A bathrobe belt, a long scarf, a dog leash, or a length of soft rope. Anything that will not stretch and will not cut into the hands.

If you are buying one

A 6-foot strap suits most people; 8 feet is worth having if you are taller than about 6 feet. A plain metal D-ring buckle is easier to work one-handed than a plastic cinch.

The blanket Padding, lift and warmth, in that order of usefulness.

What it actually does

A blanket does three jobs. Folded and sat on, it lifts the hips above the knees in any seated position, which lets the pelvis tip forward and the spine stay long without effort. Folded and placed under a joint, it pads. Unfolded and laid over you, it keeps you warm during a long rest, which matters more than people expect because bodies cool down fast when they stop moving.

How it is used on this site

  • Folded and sat on the front edge of, in every seated position.
  • Under the knees in any kneeling position, including Child's Pose and a low lunge.
  • Rolled and placed behind the knees to reduce how far the knee bends when kneeling.
  • Rolled and placed under the knees in a final rest, so the low back settles.
  • Folded under the shoulders in Bridge to reduce the angle at the neck.
  • Laid over you during any rest longer than about two minutes.

Household substitute

Any firm blanket you already own. A dense wool or cotton blanket folds into cleaner edges than a fleece one, which matters when you are sitting on it.

If you are buying one

Two, if you are buying. A traditional Mexican-style cotton blanket around 80 by 60 inches folds well and is what most US studios stock.

The bolster For supported and restorative work only.

What it actually does

A bolster fills the space under a shape so that the muscles can genuinely stop working. It is the least essential of the props here for a general practice and the most essential for a restorative one. If you only ever do standing and moving practice, you will not miss it. If you want to hold shapes for three to five minutes, it is the difference between resting and enduring.

How it is used on this site

  • Lengthwise between the thighs in a supported Child's Pose, with the torso resting on it.
  • Under the knees in a reclined position so the low back settles.
  • Under the length of the spine in a supported reclined shape, with the head resting on the far end.
  • Under the knees in Reclined Bound Angle, or one under each thigh.
  • Under the knees in a supine twist so they land on something rather than hanging.

Household substitute

A firm sofa cushion with a folded blanket laid over it, or three or four blankets folded into a firm rectangle. Bed pillows are too soft and will collapse.

If you are buying one

A rectangular bolster is more versatile than a round one. Standard US size is about 25 by 12 by 6 inches. Firmness matters more than size; a soft bolster will not hold you.

The chair A full alternative, not a substitute.

What it actually does

A chair raises the floor to a convenient height, gives a balance point, and lets the hips work in a supported position. Every sequence on this site has a chair path written into it and the entire Desk Reset is built around one. Chair versions are shown with the same weight as floor versions throughout this site because they are the same postures, worked from a different height.

How it is used on this site

  • Sat on, for every seated position and for the whole of the Desk Reset.
  • Hands on the seat, for Downward-Facing Dog and for a half forward bend.
  • Held at the back, for standing balance work including Tree Pose.
  • Heels on the seat, for a low-demand version of Bridge.
  • Feet on the seat, for a supported reclined position with the calves resting on it.

Household substitute

Any dining chair with four legs on the floor. Not a wheeled office chair, which will move underneath you.

If you are buying one

A chair with a flat, firm seat and no arms is the most useful. Folding metal chairs are what studios use because the back is open and you can thread your legs through it.

The wall The prop you already own.

What it actually does

A wall gives you a second floor, standing vertically. It removes the fear of falling in balance work, which frees up the attention you would otherwise spend bracing. It gives the back leg something definite to press into in a wide stance. And it lets the shoulders work overhead without carrying any weight, which is the single most useful adaptation for anyone new to Downward-Facing Dog.

How it is used on this site

  • Palms flat at hip height with the feet walked back, for a standing half forward bend.
  • Fingertips resting on it during Tree Pose and other balances.
  • The outer edge of the back foot pressed against it in Warrior II.
  • Back lightly against it in Mountain Pose, to feel where your weight sits.
  • Legs resting up it in Legs Up the Wall, with the hips a few inches away from the baseboard.

Household substitute

None needed. Clear about four feet of wall of any kind, ideally without a radiator, a picture rail or a light switch at shoulder height.

If you are buying one

Free. Check the wall is solid rather than a thin partition before you press hard against it.

A note on mats

A mat is not really a prop; it is a floor surface. Its job is to stop your hands and feet sliding and to put a small amount of padding between you and a hard floor. That is all it needs to do.

The three things that actually vary are thickness, grip and length. Thicker is kinder on the knees and worse for standing balance. Grip matters most if your hands sweat. Length matters if you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches, since a standard US mat is 68 inches long and your heels will hang off the end in a reclined position.

Beyond that, most mats in the $20 to $80 range do the job. We have written the longer version of this argument in choosing a first mat, which contains affiliate links disclosed at each link.

What to do if you own nothing at all

Practice on a carpet or a rug that does not slide. Put a folded bath towel under your knees for kneeling positions and a second one under your hips for seated ones. Use two hardback books where a block is suggested, a bathrobe belt where a strap is suggested, and a dining chair and a clear wall for everything else. That is a complete prop kit and it costs nothing.

The one thing to avoid is a smooth hard floor in socks. Standing positions need grip, and a slip is the most likely way to get hurt in a home practice.
