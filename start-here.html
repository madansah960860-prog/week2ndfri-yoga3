The beginner's guide

Start here. Nobody starts anywhere else.

Everything you need before a first practice: clothes, floor, space, time, your first eight positions and what to do when something hurts.

Photograph via Unsplash

What you are actually doing

Yoga, as practiced in most American homes and studios, is a set of physical positions held or moved through in time with the breath. That is the honest description. The positions ask joints to move through ranges they do not visit during a normal day, ask muscles to lengthen or to hold a load, and ask your attention to stay in one place longer than it wants to. Everything else you may have heard about it is either history, philosophy or marketing, and none of it is required in order to begin.

You do not need to be flexible. Flexibility is one of the things a practice slowly changes, not a prerequisite for having one. You do not need to be calm, coordinated, thin, young, or already good at anything. What you need is a floor, some clothes you can move in, and about ten minutes.

Beginners usually arrive with one of two anxieties. The first is that they will do it wrong. The second is that everyone else can already do it. The first is worth taking seriously and this guide addresses it directly. The second is not worth a minute of your attention, because a home practice has no audience.

What "difficulty" means on Stillpoint

Every pose entry carries a difficulty reading. It refers to how much joint range, balance and load tolerance the standard version of the shape assumes, not to how worthy the pose is. A pose marked Beginner is one most people can find a workable version of on the first day. Intermediate assumes some existing range and some familiarity with the cues. Advanced assumes both, plus time.

Every difficulty level has prop-supported versions listed alongside it, and those versions are not lesser. A supported shape held well teaches you more than an unsupported shape you are fighting.

How to read a pose entry

Each entry in the pose library is laid out the same way, so once you learn the format you can read any of them quickly:

  • Names. English first, then the Sanskrit name with diacritics, so you recognize it when a teacher uses it.
  • Entry steps. Numbered, in order, starting from a position you are already in.
  • Alignment cues. Directions, not destinations. See how to read an alignment cue.
  • Breath pattern. Where the inhale and exhale go, and whether the pose is held or moved through.
  • Suggested hold. A range in seconds or breaths. Ranges, not rules.
  • Common mistakes. The three or four things that most often go sideways.
  • Accessible modifications. At least two, using a chair, wall, block, strap, bolster or blanket.
  • Progression. Where the shape goes next, when and if you want it to.
  • Approach with care. Who should treat the pose cautiously or skip it, and when to ask a qualified teacher.

What you need, and what you do not

The honest list is very short. Everything past the first four items is convenience.

The actual list

  • Floor space, roughly 7 feet by 3 feet. Lie down and stretch your arms overhead. If nothing hits a wall or a coffee table, you have enough. Standing sequences need about a foot of clearance on each side.
  • Clothes you can move in. Anything that lets your knees and hips bend fully and does not slide up over your head when you fold forward. Bare feet. No shoes, no socks, because socks slide.
  • A mat, or a rug that does not slip. A mat costs between $20 and $120. See choosing a first mat. A dense carpet works for seated and lying work; it is poor for standing balances.
  • A timer. Your phone, on Do Not Disturb, face down. Or use the sequence player built into every sequence page on this site.

Useful, not required

  • Two blocks. The single most useful prop in the list. They raise the floor to your hand so your spine does not have to round to reach it. A stack of firm books works.
  • A strap. Extends your reach. A bathrobe belt, a dog leash or a long scarf all work.
  • A blanket. Folded under the hips in seated postures, under the knees in kneeling postures, under the head when lying down. Any firm blanket you own.
  • A bolster. Only really needed for supported and restorative work. A firm sofa cushion under a folded blanket is a serviceable substitute.
  • A sturdy chair. Not a wheeled office chair. A dining chair with four legs on the floor. Every sequence on this site has a chair path written into it.

What you genuinely do not need

Grip socks, a mat towel, a wheel, a subscription app, leggings that cost more than the mat, or anything described as a starter kit. None of it makes a beginner practice better. Buy a mat, borrow the rest from your own house, and spend the money on a class with a real teacher when you are ready for one.

Your first eight positions

Learn these eight and you can follow almost any beginner class in the United States. Each links to a full breakdown with entry steps, cues and prop options.

The eight shapes worth learning first, in the order we teach them.
Position Sanskrit What it asks of you First-day option
Mountain Pose Tāḍāsana Standing still with weight even across both feet. It is the reference position for every other standing shape. Stand with your back lightly against a wall.
Child's Pose Bālāsana Kneeling with the hips settling back toward the heels and the torso resting forward. Blanket behind the knees, bolster under the chest.
Downward-Facing Dog Adho Mukha Śvānāsana An inverted V loading the shoulders and lengthening the back of the legs. Hands on a chair seat or a wall instead of the floor.
Cat and Cow Marjaryāsana / Bitilāsana Moving the spine into flexion and extension on all fours, timed to the breath. Do it seated in a chair with hands on the knees.
Warrior II Vīrabhadrāsana II A wide stance with the front knee bent and the arms level. Shorten the stance by a foot. Bend the knee less.
Tree Pose Vṛkṣāsana Standing on one leg with the other foot resting against the standing leg. Fingertips on a wall. Toes of the lifted foot on the floor.
Bridge Pose Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana Lying on your back, lifting the hips with the feet planted. Place a block under the hips and simply rest there.
Reclined Rest Śavāsana Lying still on your back at the end of a practice, doing nothing at all. Blanket roll under the knees. Blanket over you.

How long, and how often

Ten minutes, four or five days a week, beats sixty minutes once a week by a wide margin. Not because ten minutes is magic, but because ten minutes is a promise you will keep in a week where everything goes wrong, and sixty is not.

A reasonable first month looks like this:

  • Week one. The 10-Minute Morning three times. Read one pose entry before each practice.
  • Week two. Same sequence four times. Add the Desk Reset on any day you sat for more than six hours.
  • Week three. Add the Evening Wind-Down twice. You are now practicing five days a week without having planned to.
  • Week four. Try the Gentle Full Practice once, on the day you have the most room in your calendar. Keep the short ones for everything else.

Time of day does not matter nearly as much as consistency of trigger. Attach the practice to something that already happens every day. After the coffee is poured. Before the shower. When the laptop closes. See building a home practice for the longer version of this argument.

What a practice is not

It is not a workout you owe yourself, and it is not a moral event. Skipping a day is not a failure with consequences; it is a day you did not practice. The practice does not keep score and neither should you.

It is also not a weight or body-shape project. Stillpoint does not publish calorie figures, before-and-after imagery, body commentary or anything about diets, cleanses or fasting, and it never will. What a posture does is physical and describable: a joint moves, a muscle lengthens, a load is carried. That is the whole subject.

Signs the practice is working

You get onto the floor without a negotiation. You notice how you are sitting at a desk. You know the name of the shape you are in. You can hold a position for a full minute without checking a clock. None of those are dramatic, and all of them are the point.

When something hurts

Stop. That is the whole instruction, and it is not negotiable.

There is a difference between the broad, dull, spread-out sensation of a muscle being lengthened, which fades within seconds when you come out of the shape, and a sharp, pinching, burning, electric or joint-located sensation, which does not. The first is ordinary. The second is a signal to come out of the position immediately, and not to push through it.

Do not practice through pain, and do not treat pain as something to be overcome by breathing differently or by trying harder. If a sensation persists after you leave the shape, if it appears in a joint rather than a muscle, if it travels down a limb, or if it is still present the next day, stop practicing and speak with a qualified healthcare professional.

Stillpoint cannot assess you, and it does not try to. We cover general movement education. We do not diagnose, treat or give medical advice, and nothing on this site should be read as a claim that a posture or a breathing pattern addresses any medical condition. Before you begin a new physical practice, and particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, recovering from surgery, or managing any health condition, speak with a qualified healthcare professional who can actually see you.

Questions beginners ask us

Longer answers to more of these sit on the FAQ page.

Yes, and the ability to touch your toes is not a qualification for anything. Hamstring length varies enormously between people and is partly a matter of skeletal proportion, which does not change. Every forward fold on Stillpoint is listed with a bent-knee version and a block version, and both are the real pose. Put two blocks under your hands and fold to those instead of to the floor.

Most classes suggest nose breathing because it naturally slows the breath and makes it audible enough to keep time with. It is a convention, not a rule. If your nose is blocked, or nose breathing makes you anxious, breathe through your mouth. A breath you can sustain comfortably is better than a breath you are fighting. The breathwork basics page covers this in detail and includes an on-screen count you can practice with.

Fold a blanket and place it under the knees, or roll a blanket and place it in the crease behind the knee to reduce how far the joint bends. If kneeling remains uncomfortable, use the chair version listed on every kneeling pose entry; it is a genuine alternative, not a lesser one. Persistent knee pain is a reason to speak with a qualified healthcare professional rather than to keep experimenting.

Check three things: can you breathe evenly in the shape, is the sensation broad rather than sharp, and can you come out of it under control. If all three are true, you are close enough for a home practice. For anything more precise than that you need a qualified teacher in the room with you, because alignment feedback requires somebody to see you. The teacher directory is a starting point for finding one.

Regular practice generally increases the range through which you can comfortably move a joint, and how much depends on where you are starting, your skeletal structure, your age, your other activities and how consistently you practice. We are not going to give you a number or a timeline, because anyone who does is guessing.

Both, if you can. A home practice builds the habit, which is the hard part. A class with a qualified teacher gives you the thing a website structurally cannot: somebody watching you move and telling you what they see. Many US studios offer a discounted first class or a beginner series. Use this site to prepare for that room, not to replace it.
