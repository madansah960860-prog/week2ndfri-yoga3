Styles explained

Six styles, described without the marketing.

What actually happens in the room, how fast it moves, how long it lasts, which props appear, and what to expect on a first visit.

Photograph via Unsplash

Reading a class listing

Studio listings in the United States tend to describe styles in terms of how they will make you feel, which is not useful information when you are deciding whether to walk into a room. This page describes them the other way around: what the teacher does, how fast things move, how long you are there, and what props you will be handed.

Style names are also used loosely. A class called Hatha at one studio may be almost identical to a class called Slow Flow at another. If a listing is ambiguous, the two questions worth asking at the desk are how long the postures are held for, and whether props are provided.

Nothing on this page describes what a style does for you beyond the physical, because that is not a claim we can make. What we can describe is the room, the pace and the format.

The six styles

Listed roughly from the slowest and most instructional to the most vigorous.

Hatha

Pace Slow. Postures held, with pauses between them. Typical length 60 to 75 minutes

What happens in the room

In the United States, a class labeled Hatha is usually a general-pace class in which postures are taught one at a time, held for several breaths, and separated by a pause. The teacher will typically demonstrate, then walk the room. There is no set order and no expectation that you already know the shapes.

As a beginner

It is the most useful first class for most people, because the pace leaves room for instructions and questions. Nothing moves faster than you can follow. Expect standing work, some floor work, and a rest at the end.

Worth knowing

You will hear postures named in both English and Sanskrit, and you will usually get more detail per posture than in any other style on this list except Iyengar.

Props typically used

Blocks and straps are common.

Vinyasa

Pace Continuous. Movement linked to the breath. Typical length 45 to 75 minutes

What happens in the room

Vinyasa means the postures are strung together and you move from one to the next on an inhale or an exhale rather than pausing between them. Classes usually build to a peak posture and then wind down. The room is often warm.

As a beginner

It is physically demanding in a cardiovascular sense, and beginners frequently find the pace faster than the instruction. If you are new, look for a class described as slow flow, beginner flow, or Vinyasa 1.

Worth knowing

You will pass through Downward-Facing Dog many times. Teachers usually offer alternatives out loud, and taking Child's Pose at any point is completely normal.

Props typically used

A mat and a towel. Props appear less often.

Yin

Pace Very slow. Long holds, mostly seated and lying. Typical length 60 to 90 minutes

What happens in the room

Yin classes hold a small number of floor postures for two to five minutes each, with props used generously so the shape can be maintained without muscular effort. There is almost no standing work and very little movement between shapes.

As a beginner

The long holds are the point. What is unfamiliar for most beginners is not the physical demand, which is low, but the length of time spent in one position, which asks a different kind of attention.

Worth knowing

Postures often have different names in Yin than in other styles. Ask the teacher which shape they mean if a name is unfamiliar; nobody minds.

Props typically used

Bolsters, blocks and blankets throughout.

Restorative

Pace Still. Fully supported shapes held for minutes at a time. Typical length 60 to 75 minutes

What happens in the room

Restorative classes use a large quantity of props to hold the body in comfortable positions for five to twenty minutes at a time. There is essentially no muscular effort involved. A class may cover only four or five shapes in an hour.

As a beginner

It is the most accessible style on this list and it is appropriate for almost anyone, including complete beginners and people returning to movement after a long gap. Studios often provide the props, which is worth checking before you go.

Worth knowing

Bring an extra layer. Bodies cool down quickly when they stop moving, and the shapes are held for a long time.

Props typically used

Bolsters, blankets, blocks, straps and eye covers.

Iyengar

Pace Precise. Detailed instruction, long holds, heavy prop use. Typical length 75 to 90 minutes

What happens in the room

Iyengar classes are built around precise alignment instruction and the systematic use of props to make each posture available to each body. A single standing posture may be taught for ten minutes. Teachers in this tradition complete a long, formal training.

As a beginner

It is exacting and it is unusually explicit about what to do, which many beginners find reassuring rather than intimidating. If you have wondered what a cue actually means, this is the style most likely to answer.

Worth knowing

Expect to use a wall, a chair and several blankets in a single class. Props here are structural rather than optional.

Props typically used

Blocks, straps, blankets, chairs, bolsters and wall ropes.

Ashtanga

Pace Vigorous. A fixed sequence, practiced the same way each time. Typical length 60 to 90 minutes

What happens in the room

Ashtanga follows a set series of postures in a fixed order. In a Mysore-style room, everyone practices their own portion of the series at their own pace while a teacher moves around the room. In a led class, the teacher counts the whole room through it together.

As a beginner

It is physically demanding and the fixed order means there is a lot to memorize early on. Most studios ask beginners to attend a led beginner class or an introductory course before joining a Mysore room.

Worth knowing

The repetition is the method: practicing the same sequence repeatedly is how the detail becomes visible. It suits people who like structure and dislike surprises.

Props typically used

A mat and a towel.
